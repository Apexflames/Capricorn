Unit 1

Introduction to Women's History

Why women were "hidden from history," and the tools and theories scholars use to bring them back.

What the Course Means & Covers

Source
Women in History is a course that studies the lives, work, struggles and achievements of women from the earliest times up to the 21st century... It is also called women's history. It is not a separate story from "real" history. It is the same history, told with women included.

The course looks at five main things: Women's roles (home, work, religion, politics, war, culture), Women's status (rights — property, voting, study, work, marriage choice), Women's struggles (fights against unfair rules), Women's achievements (queens, warriors, writers, scientists, ordinary women), and Change over time.

What a historian asksWho wrote this record, and was it a man or a woman? Whose voice is missing? Did all women have the same experience (rich/poor, African/European)? What caused change, and who pushed for it?

The Problem of the Missing Women

Source
The real question is not "Did women make history?" The real question is "Why did the history books leave them out?"

In 1973, British historian Sheila Rowbotham wrote Hidden from History — women were not absent from the past; they were hidden from the telling of it.

Why Were Women Left Out? (5 reasons)

  1. Definition of history itself — Leopold von Ranke built history on official state documents (made by men); Thomas Carlyle's "great man theory" said history is shaped by kings, generals and prophets.
  2. Patriarchy — a system where men hold most power, so men's work/words were valued and recorded more.
  3. Women's work seen as "ordinary" — childcare, cooking, caregiving were "natural duties," not "events" ("reproductive labour"/"care work").
  4. Lack of education — fewer women could read/write, so fewer letters, diaries and legal papers survive.
  5. Deliberate erasure — e.g. Hatshepsut's statues broken and name cut from monuments after her death; Rosalind Franklin's DNA work ("Photo 51") credited to others — Margaret Rossiter named this the "Matilda effect" (1993).

How the Field Developed

Early voices before it was a subject: Christine de Pizan (The Book of the City of Ladies, 1405), Olympe de Gouges (Declaration of the Rights of Woman, 1791 — executed 1793), Mary Wollstonecraft (A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, 1792), Virginia Woolf ("Shakespeare's sister," 1929), Mary Ritter Beard (1946), Simone de Beauvoir (The Second Sex, 1949).

It became a university field in the 1960s–70s, driven by second-wave feminism and "history from below" (E. P. Thompson, 1963). Gerda Lerner helped start one of the first graduate programmes (Sarah Lawrence, 1972) and named two early stages: "compensatory history" (finding the forgotten "women worthies") and "contribution history" (what women contributed to movements men already wrote about). She said both were necessary but not enough — the deeper goal is to rethink history with women's experience at the centre.

By 1986, Joan Wallach Scott's "Gender: A Useful Category of Historical Analysis" pushed the field toward studying gender — how societies give meaning to male/female differences and turn that into systems of power — creating gender history alongside women's history.

Theories on the Roots of Patriarchy

Compare the theories

The African Challenge

Source
Much early women's history came from Europe and America, and it often treated Western experience as if it were universal. African scholars have strongly challenged this.
  • Ifi Amadiume, Male Daughters, Female Husbands (1987) — Igbo gender roles were flexible; colonial rule and Christianity made divisions stricter.
  • Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí, The Invention of Women (1997) — pre-colonial Yoruba society ranked people by seniority, not gender; "woman" as a lower category came with colonial rule.
  • Aba Women's War (1929) — thousands of Igbo women protested British colonial rule using "sitting on a man" (Judith Van Allen, 1972).
  • African feminisms: womanism (Alice Walker, 1983), African womanism (Chikwenye Okonjo Ogunyemi), nego-feminism (Obioma Nnaemeka).

Sources & Methods

Historians read written sources "against the grain" (Natalie Zemon Davis), use oral history/tradition (especially vital in Africa — Jan Vansina), archaeology/material evidence (e.g. the 2017 Birka Viking grave DNA result), and must confront silence (Gayatri Spivak's "Can the Subaltern Speak?", 1988).

Key Terms Explained Deeply

TermMeaning
Sex vs. GenderSex = biological difference. Gender = roles/meanings society attaches to being male/female (Ann Oakley, 1972; Gayle Rubin's "sex/gender system," 1975; Judith Butler — gender as "performed," 1990)
PatriarchyGreek for "rule of the father" — a system where men (esp. older men) hold most power
FeminismBelief/movement for equal rights. Waves: 1st (voting/legal rights), 2nd (1960s–70s, work/family/body), 3rd (1990s–, difference/identity)
Equality vs. EquityEquality = treating people the same. Equity = giving people what they need to be treated fairly
IntersectionalityKimberlé Crenshaw (1989) — a person's experience is shaped by several identity parts at once (gender, race, class, religion)
AgencyA person's ability to make choices and act, even under limits

Does History Have the Same Periods for Women?

Source
Joan Kelly's 1977 essay "Did Women Have a Renaissance?" — her answer for Renaissance Italy was "no." A "golden age" for men may not be a golden age for women.

Major Debates

  • Victims vs. agents — were women mostly controlled, or did they also act and resist? (Mary Beard argued for agency; critics say this can hide real injustice.)
  • Universal vs. local — is women's position the same everywhere, or does it vary by culture?
  • Difference vs. equality — treat men/women exactly alike, or value women's different experiences?
  • Is "women" a useful category at all? — class, race, nation and religion may divide women too much to speak of one group.

Likely Exam Questions

Likely Question

Q: Why were women left out of traditional history books? Give three reasons with examples.

(1) History was defined around official state documents and the "great man theory" (Ranke, Carlyle), which excluded women by definition. (2) Patriarchy meant men's work and words were valued and recorded more. (3) Women's achievements were sometimes deliberately erased — e.g. Hatshepsut's monuments defaced, or Rosalind Franklin's DNA work credited to others (the "Matilda effect," Rossiter 1993).
📍 See Unit 1 → Why Were Women Left Out?
Likely Question

Q: Explain Gerda Lerner's distinction between "compensatory history" and "contribution history."

Compensatory history finds the forgotten "women worthies" — queens, writers, scientists — missed by traditional history. Contribution history asks what women contributed to movements men already wrote about (wars, revolutions, reforms). Lerner said both were necessary but insufficient, because they still measure women against the existing male-centred story; the deeper goal is to rethink history with women's experience at the centre.

Unit 2

Women in Ancient Civilizations (c. 3000 BCE – 500 CE)

Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome, India, China and ancient Africa — compared.

The Problem of Evidence & Two Big Theories

Most ancient writing came from rich, educated men, often describing how women should behave, not how they lived. Historians use laws, letters, tombs, inscriptions — mostly about elite women.

  • Matriarchy theory — Bachofen (1861) and Gimbutas (1970s–80s) argued early societies were ruled by women / worshipped a Great Goddess. Most historians today reject this: no strong evidence a true matriarchy ever existed.
  • Gerda Lerner's theory — patriarchy was built slowly in the ancient Near East (c. 3100–600 BCE), through control of women's sexuality/children, marriage exchange, enslavement of captured women as a model for slavery, and laws tying a woman's status to her father/husband.

Region-by-Region Snapshot

RegionKey points
EgyptStrongest legal/economic rights of the ancient world — women could own property, divorce, go to court without a guardian (Naunakhte's will, c.1150 BCE). Female pharaoh Hatshepsut (c.1479–1458 BCE); Cleopatra VII (51–30 BCE); earliest woman doctor Peseshet; "God's Wife of Amun" religious office.
MesopotamiaWriting began c.3200 BCE. Enheduanna (c.2285 BCE) — first named author in history. Code of Hammurabi gave dowry rights but harsh adultery laws. Middle Assyrian Laws required "respectable" women to veil (Lerner's key example of class among women). The naditu of Sippar — cloistered women who owned property.
Greece (Athens)Women under a male guardian (kyrios) their whole lives; no property rights; ideal was staying unseen ("least spoken about," Pericles). Sparta was different — women trained physically, owned ~2/5 of land (Aristotle).
RomeUpper-class women gained real legal independence over time (no manus marriage by late Republic); could own property, divorce easily. Six Vestal Virgins held unique honour. Could not vote or hold office.
IndiaEarly Vedic period had women philosophers (Gargi Vachaknavi). Later Manusmriti (c.200 BCE–200 CE) placed women under father/husband/son. Buddhism opened a path via nuns (bhikkhunis), after Mahapajapati Gotami's request.
ChinaFu Hao (d. c.1200 BCE) — Shang queen, military general and priestess. Confucian "Three Obediences" (father → husband → son). Ban Zhao (c.45–116 CE) completed the Book of Han and wrote Lessons for Women, yet argued girls should be educated.
Africa (Kush)The Kandake queens of Meroe. Amanirenas (c.40–10 BCE) led the Kushite army against Rome and won favourable peace terms (c.21–20 BCE).

Going Deeper (Unit 2 Expanded)

Click to expand — before the cities, new evidence, and extra scholars

Before the cities: "Man the hunter" (1968 conference) was challenged by Sally Slocum's 1975 essay "Woman the Gatherer" — gathered food may have provided most daily calories. A 2020 Andes grave (Wilamaya Patjxa) with a woman buried alongside hunting tools reopened debate on women hunters. Conkey & Gero's Engendering Archaeology (1991): always ask what the evidence says vs. what we assume.

Precious women's voices: the Vindolanda tablets (Claudia Severa's birthday letter, c.100 CE) — earliest known Latin writing by a woman. Sulpicia's Roman love poems. Perpetua's prison diary (Carthage, 203 CE) — one of the earliest Christian texts by a woman; she and the slave Felicity were martyred.

Deeper Egypt: early queens Merneith and Khentkaus I; Queen Tiye's international correspondence (Amarna letters); Nefertiti's bust; the Kahun Gynecological Papyrus (c.1800 BCE) — oldest medical text on women's health in the world. Janet Johnson: Egyptian women's legal power did not depend on a male guardian.

Deeper Mesopotamia: the myth of Inanna's descent to the underworld; Queen Shibtu who ran the palace at Mari; regents Sammu-ramat and Naqi'a. Stephanie Budin's The Myth of Sacred Prostitution in Antiquity (2008) challenges the old "sacred prostitute" claim.

Deeper Greece: the Pandora myth (Hesiod, c.700 BCE) blames the "first woman"; the trial of Neaira (c.340 BCE); the "wandering womb" medical idea; Cynisca, first woman Olympic winner (as horse owner, 396/392 BCE); Artemisia of Caria fought for Persia at Salamis (480 BCE).

Deeper Rome: the legend of Lucretia and the fall of the kings; Fulvia led a war against Octavian (41 BCE); Zenobia of Palmyra challenged Rome (267–272 CE); the inscription praising "Turia" for her courage during the civil wars.

Deeper India: the Indus Valley "Dancing Girl" figurine (undeciphered script); Sanghamitta (Ashoka's daughter) carried the Bodhi tree to Sri Lanka; Tamil Sangam poetry includes c.30 women poets (e.g. Avvaiyar).

Deeper China: the Book of Songs (Shijing); the widow Ba Qing, honoured by Qin Shi Huang for her cinnabar-mining business; Sima Qian gave Empress Lü her own chapter in the Shiji; The Ballad of Mulan (legend, Northern Wei period).

Deeper Africa: Amanishakheto's treasure (looted 1834, now in Berlin/Munich museums); the Dido/Carthage legend; Perpetua's story again, as a North African Christian martyr.

Unit 3

Women in the Medieval World (c. 500–1500 CE)

Europe, the Islamic world, Asia and Africa — a thousand years, four regions.

The Problem of Evidence & Key Scholars

Sources include manor court rolls, letters (the Paston Letters), saints' lives and mystics' visions, Books of Hours, tax/guild lists, and travellers' accounts (Ibn Battuta on Mali, 1352–53; Zhou Daguan on Angkor, 1296–97). Founding scholar: Eileen Power (Medieval Women, 1975). Later: Shulamith Shahar, Caroline Walker Bynum, Judith Bennett; for Islam: Leila Ahmed, Fatima Mernissi.

PART ONE — Medieval Europe

  • Eve vs. Mary — the Church's double image of woman: sinner (Eve) vs. impossible ideal (Mary, Queen of Heaven).
  • Peasant women (the majority) — field work, brewing (Judith Bennett's "brewsters"), manor-court fines like leyrwite and merchet.
  • Townswomen — the femme sole rule let a married trader act independently despite coverture; women's-only guilds existed in Cologne/Paris. Widows held dower rights.
  • Marriage law changed — by the 1100s, the Church required free consent of both parties (Peter Lombard, Pope Alexander III).
  • Noblewomen & queens — Eleanor of Aquitaine (c.1122–1204, queen of both France and England); Empress Matilda denied the English crown; France's Salic law barred women from the throne (helped cause the Hundred Years' War).
  • Women of the Church — Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179, abbess/composer/writer, Doctor of the Church 2012); Heloise; the Beguines (Marguerite Porete burned 1310); mystics Catherine of Siena, Julian of Norwich, Margery Kempe.
  • Writers — Christine de Pizan (1364–c.1430), first to argue publicly against literary insults to women; The Book of the City of Ladies (1405).
  • Joan of Arc (c.1412–1431) — led the French army, crowned Charles VII, burned for heresy (clothing was a central charge), made a saint in 1920.

PART TWO — The Medieval Islamic World

  • Khadija — wealthy trader, the Prophet's first wife and first believer.
  • Aisha bint Abi Bakr — taught ~2,200 hadith; took part in the Battle of the Camel (656).
  • The Qur'an gave women rights to own/inherit property, a mahr (bride's own gift), consent in marriage, and khul' divorce — but some rules (inheritance shares, witness testimony) were unequal.
  • Debate: Leila Ahmed — early ethical message vs. later stricter legal rules under Abbasid rulers; Fatima Mernissi — some hadith re-examined and challenged (The Veil and the Male Elite, 1987; The Forgotten Queens of Islam, 1990).
  • Women scholars: over 8,000 recorded hadith teachers (Mohammad Akram Nadwi's Al-Muhaddithat, 2007).
  • Ruling queens: Arwa al-Sulayhi (Yemen), Shajar al-Durr (Egypt, 1250), Razia Sultana (Delhi, 1236–1240).

PART THREE — Medieval Asia

RegionKey points
ChinaWu Zetian (624–705) — only woman to rule China as emperor in her own name. Song dynasty: Neo-Confucianism, widow chastity, and foot binding began. Poet Li Qingzhao (1084–c.1155).
Japan (Heian)Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji (often called the first novel); Sei Shōnagon wrote The Pillow Book. Women wrote in Japanese kana script, seen as the "women's hand."
IndiaBhakti poets gave women religious voice (Akka Mahadevi, Mirabai). Rani Rudrama Devi ruled the Kakatiya dynasty (1262–1289). Sati and purdah existed but were not universal.
Mongol EmpireSorghaghtani Beki raised four sons who became great rulers, including Kublai Khan.

PART FOUR — Medieval Africa

  • Epic of Sundiata — Sogolon, the hero's mother, central to the founding of the Mali Empire (c.1235).
  • In Ghana (the old empire), kingship passed through the sister's son (matrilineal succession) — al-Bakri, 1068.
  • Ibn Battuta (1352–53) was shocked by the freedom of women in Walata, Mali — a useful reminder of outsider bias in sources.
  • Queen mothers held real titles and power: the Magira (Kanem-Borno), the Iyoba (Benin, created for Idia), the Ohemaa (Akan).
  • Warrior queens: Kahina (North Africa, d. c.703) resisted Arab conquest; Amina of Zazzau (Hausa legend/tradition).

Was the Medieval World Good or Bad for Women? (4 views, as supplied)

  1. Better than what came after — Eileen Power, Alice Clark: medieval towns offered more work than later centuries.
  2. Always under men's control — laws, Church teaching and male-held top offices show real limits.
  3. Little changed — Judith Bennett's "patriarchal equilibrium": despite plagues, wars and new trades, men's basic power stayed steady.
  4. It depends on who, where and when — the most widely accepted view today; a nun, a peasant, a queen and a trader lived completely different lives.

Going Deeper (Unit 3 Expanded)

Click to expand — Paston women, Church courts, and region-by-region depth

The Paston Women (England, 1448–1469): Margaret Paston ran farms, defended the manor under armed attack, and wrote over 100 surviving letters. Her daughter Margery married the family's estate manager, Richard Calle, against her family's wishes — and the bishop of Norwich upheld the marriage because Church law only required free consent of both parties.

The Church's "double" view: Marina Warner's Alone of All Her Sex (1976) on the Virgin Mary cult. Georges Duby's The Knight, the Lady and the Priest (1983) — noble families wanted marriage for land/power; the Church insisted on consent and permanence.

Village and town women, closer up: Barbara Hanawalt used coroners' rolls to trace women's daily dangers (The Ties That Bound, 1986). Martha Howell argued that as work moved from household to workshop, women's recognised skill shrank (1986); Kowaleski & Bennett (1989) countered that guilds did not always exclude women.

Noblewomen at war: "Black Agnes" (Agnes Randolph) defended Dunbar Castle for five months (1338); Jeanne de Montfort defended Hennebont in Brittany.

Deeper Islamic world: Nusayba bint Ka'b fought at the Battle of Uhud (625); Zaynab bint Ali spoke out after Karbala (680); the hadith "A people who entrust their affairs to a woman will not prosper" is challenged by Mernissi as politically motivated, tied to the Battle of the Camel. Waqf (religious endowments) were often funded by wealthy women.

Deeper Asia: Empress Dowager Xiao (Liao dynasty, regent 27 years); the Kamakura law code of 1232 let Japanese women inherit land, but these rights shrank by the 1300s–1400s (Haruko Wakita); Queen Didda ruled Kashmir for nearly 50 years.

Deeper Africa: Tin Hinan, legendary Tuareg queen — in Tuareg society it is men who veil, not women; griots and jelimuso (female griots) keep oral history alive; the Yoruba legend of Moremi Ajasoro, who sacrificed her only son to save Ife.

The big debates, slowed down: James C. Scott's idea of "everyday resistance" (Weapons of the Weak, 1985) — small, quiet acts of defiance (Hildegard's "weak woman" framing, Christine's storytelling, village women's continued brewing despite fines) count as agency even when not loud.
